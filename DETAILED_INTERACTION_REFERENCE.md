## Interaction 1: Ibuprofen & Naproxen

**Severity**: HIGH  
**Drug Classes**: NSAID + NSAID  
**Key Risk**: Increased risk of severe gastrointestinal bleeding and kidney damage

### Active Ingredients to Enter
- Drug 1: `Ibuprofen` (brand names: Advil, Motrin, Nurofen)
- Drug 2: `Naproxen` (brand names: Aleve, Naprosyn)

### Context
Both ibuprofen and naproxen are non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) commonly used for pain relief, fever, and inflammation. They are among the most widely used OTC medications in Jordan. A person might take ibuprofen for a headache and later take naproxen for muscle pain, not realizing both belong to the same drug class.

### Pharmacological Mechanism
**The Interaction**: Additive Toxicity

Both drugs work by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, which produce prostaglandins responsible for pain and inflammation. When taken together:
- The body registers total COX enzyme inhibition
- This is equivalent to an overdose of a single NSAID
- Side effects are dramatically amplified
- No therapeutic benefit from combining them

### Clinical Risks

**1. Gastrointestinal Damage** (Most Common)
- Severe stomach irritation
- Peptic ulcers
- Life-threatening GI bleeding
- **Symptoms**: Severe stomach pain, vomiting blood (coffee-ground appearance), black or tarry stools

**2. Kidney Damage**
- Reduced blood flow to kidneys
- Acute kidney injury
- Fluid retention
- Elevated blood pressure
- Risk increases with dehydration or existing kidney disease

**3. Cardiovascular Risk**
- Increased risk of heart attack
- Increased risk of stroke
- Elevated blood pressure
- Risk amplified in those with heart disease

### Expert Recommendations

**DO:**
- Choose ONE NSAID and stick to it
- Read labels on all OTC products (many cold/flu remedies contain NSAIDs)
- If one NSAID doesn't work, consult a pharmacist
- Consider paracetamol (acetaminophen) as an alternative - it works differently

**DON'T:**
- Never combine two NSAIDs
- Don't assume "different brand = safe to combine"
- Don't exceed recommended doses
- Avoid if you have history of ulcers or kidney disease

**Safe Alternative**: Combine an NSAID with paracetamol for better pain relief without increased toxicity.

## Interaction 2: Antacids & Iron Supplements

**Severity**: MODERATE to HIGH  
**Drug Classes**: Antacid + Mineral Supplement  
**Key Risk**: Reduced iron absorption, treatment failure for anemia

### Active Ingredients to Enter
- Drug 1: `Antacid` (containing calcium, magnesium, or aluminum)
- Drug 2: `Iron` or `Ferrous Sulfate` (iron supplements)

### Context
Antacids are household staples for heartburn and indigestion. Iron supplements are prescribed for anemia. Someone with iron-deficiency anemia who also has heartburn might unknowingly take both medications together, rendering the iron therapy ineffective.

### Pharmacological Mechanism
**The Interaction**: pH-Dependent Absorption Failure + Chelation

**Two-Way Mechanism**:
1. **pH Change**: Antacids neutralize stomach acid, raising gastric pH. Iron requires an acidic environment to convert into an absorbable form (ferrous state).
2. **Chelation**: Mineral ions in antacids (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺) directly bind to iron, forming large insoluble complexes that cannot be absorbed and are excreted in stool.

### Clinical Risks

**1. Treatment Failure**
- Iron therapy becomes ineffective
- Anemia persists or worsens
- Continued fatigue, weakness, shortness of breath
- Pale skin, dizziness, cold hands/feet

**2. Economic Waste**
- Patient continues buying supplements with no benefit
- Delayed resolution of medical condition
- May lead to unnecessary medical investigations

**3. Potential Complications**
- Severe anemia if untreated
- Pregnancy complications (if anemic during pregnancy)
- Reduced quality of life

### Expert Recommendations

**DO:**
- **Separate doses by timing**: Take iron 2+ hours BEFORE or 4+ hours AFTER antacid
- Set reminders to maintain the timing
- Check multivitamins - many contain iron
- Inform pharmacist about all medications

**DON'T:**
- Never take antacid and iron together
- Don't take iron with milk (same chelation issue)
- Don't assume "a little gap" is enough

**Pro Tip**: Take iron on an empty stomach with vitamin C (orange juice) for maximum absorption, then wait 4 hours before taking antacid.

## Interaction 3: Bisacodyl & Antacids

**Severity**: LOW to MODERATE  
**Drug Classes**: Stimulant Laxative + Antacid  
**Key Risk**: Premature tablet dissolution, stomach irritation

### Active Ingredients to Enter
- Drug 1: `Bisacodyl` (stimulant laxative)
- Drug 2: `Antacid`

### Context
Bisacodyl is used for constipation. Someone might take it along with an antacid for unrelated heartburn, not knowing the antacid affects how the laxative tablet works.

### Pharmacological Mechanism
**The Interaction**: Pharmaceutical/Formulation Interaction

**How Bisacodyl Works**:
- Stimulates intestinal muscles
- Coated with "enteric coating" (acid-resistant)
- Designed to dissolve only in the alkaline environment of small intestine
- Protects stomach from irritation

**What Antacids Do**:
- Neutralize stomach acid
- Create alkaline environment in stomach
- "Tricks" enteric coating into dissolving prematurely
- Releases bisacodyl in stomach instead of intestine

### Clinical Risks

**1. Gastric Distress**
- Direct stomach irritation
- Nausea and indigestion
- Painful stomach cramps
- Heartburn (ironic, since taking antacid!)

**2. Reduced Effectiveness**
- Drug released in wrong location
- May not provide expected laxative effect
- Constipation persists

### Expert Recommendations

**DO:**
- Maintain 1-hour separation (before or after antacid)
- Same rule applies to milk and PPIs (omeprazole)
- Swallow tablets whole - never crush or chew enteric-coated tablets

**DON'T:**
- Don't take within 1 hour of antacid
- Don't take with milk
- Don't break or chew the tablet

## Interaction 4: Diphenhydramine & Chlorpheniramine

**Severity**: MODERATE to HIGH  
**Drug Classes**: Sedating Antihistamine + Sedating Antihistamine  
**Key Risk**: Severe drowsiness, impaired coordination, accident risk

### Active Ingredients to Enter
- Drug 1: `Diphenhydramine` (found in Benadryl, sleep aids, PM formulas)
- Drug 2: `Chlorpheniramine` (found in cold/allergy medicines)

### Context
Both are first-generation antihistamines found in many OTC products. A person might take a cold remedy with chlorpheniramine during the day, then a "PM" sleep aid with diphenhydramine at night, doubling up on sedating antihistamines.

### Pharmacological Mechanism
**The Interaction**: Additive CNS Depression

**How These Drugs Work**:
- Block histamine receptors for allergy relief
- Cross blood-brain barrier readily
- Block brain histamine receptors → drowsiness
- Both cause significant sedation

**When Combined**:
- Sedative effects multiply
- Much greater CNS depression
- Impaired cognitive and motor function

### Clinical Risks

**1. Excessive Sedation**
- Profound drowsiness and fatigue
- Difficulty staying awake
- Dizziness and confusion
- "Hangover" effect next day

**2. Impaired Function**
- Slowed reaction times
- Poor coordination
- Increased fall risk (especially elderly)
- **Dangerous for driving or operating machinery**

**3. Cognitive Impairment**
- Memory problems
- Poor concentration
- Difficulty making decisions
- "Brain fog"

**4. Anticholinergic Effects**
- Dry mouth
- Blurred vision
- Constipation
- Urinary retention
- Confusion (especially in elderly)

### Expert Recommendations

**DO:**
- Read labels carefully - check ALL active ingredients
- Avoid multiple "nighttime" or "PM" products
- If you must take one, avoid driving/operating machinery

**DON'T:**
- Never combine two sedating antihistamines
- Don't add alcohol (triples the sedation)
- Don't drive after taking

**Better Alternative**: Use non-sedating antihistamines (loratadine, cetirizine) during the day.

## Interaction 5: Pseudoephedrine & Caffeine

**Severity**: MODERATE  
**Drug Classes**: Decongestant + Stimulant  
**Key Risk**: Increased heart rate, blood pressure, anxiety, insomnia

### Active Ingredients to Enter
- Drug 1: `Pseudoephedrine` (nasal decongestant)
- Drug 2: `Caffeine` (coffee, tea, energy drinks, some medications)

### Context
Extremely common scenario: taking a cold medicine with pseudoephedrine while drinking morning coffee. Both are stimulants.

### Pharmacological Mechanism
**The Interaction**: Additive Stimulant Effects

**How Each Works**:
- **Pseudoephedrine**: Sympathomimetic - constricts nasal blood vessels, increases heart rate/BP
- **Caffeine**: CNS stimulant - increases alertness, heart rate, BP

**Combined Effect**: Overstimulation of cardiovascular and nervous systems

### Clinical Risks

**1. Cardiovascular Effects**
- Rapid heart rate (tachycardia)
- Heart palpitations
- Significantly elevated blood pressure
- **High risk for those with hypertension or heart disease**

**2. CNS Overstimulation**
- Extreme nervousness
- Anxiety and restlessness
- Jitteriness
- Tremors

**3. Sleep Disturbance**
- Severe insomnia
- Difficulty falling asleep
- Restless sleep

### Expert Recommendations

**DO:**
- Monitor and reduce caffeine while taking pseudoephedrine
- Avoid afternoon/evening doses
- Use decaf beverages
- **If you have heart disease/hypertension - consult doctor first**

**DON'T:**
- Don't drink multiple caffeinated beverages while on decongestants
- Don't take in evening (insomnia risk)
- Don't ignore heart palpitations

**Alternative**: Use saline nasal spray instead of pseudoephedrine.

## Interaction 6: Loperamide & Cimetidine

**Severity**: HIGH  
**Drug Classes**: Anti-diarrheal + H2 Blocker  
**Key Risk**: Dangerous cardiac side effects

### Active Ingredients to Enter
- Drug 1: `Loperamide` (Imodium, anti-diarrheal)
- Drug 2: `Cimetidine` (Tagamet, acid reducer)

### Context
Someone with diarrhea AND upset stomach might take both. This is a serious metabolic drug interaction.

### Pharmacological Mechanism
**The Interaction**: Metabolic Enzyme Inhibition

**Loperamide Metabolism**:
- Broken down by liver enzyme CYP3A4
- Normally kept out of brain by P-glycoprotein
- At normal doses, no CNS effects

**Cimetidine's Effect**:
- **Potent CYP3A4 inhibitor**
- Blocks loperamide breakdown
- Loperamide levels increase 2-3x
- Higher risk of crossing into brain

### Clinical Risks

**1. Cardiac Toxicity** (MOST SERIOUS)
- QT interval prolongation
- Dangerous arrhythmias (Torsades de Pointes)
- **Sudden cardiac arrest**
- Risk directly related to drug concentration

**2. Increased Side Effects**
- Severe constipation
- Nausea and dizziness
- Drowsiness
- CNS effects (at very high levels)

### Expert Recommendations

**DO:**
- **AVOID this combination completely**
- Use alternative acid reducers: Famotidine (Pepcid), Ranitidine, or antacids
- Never exceed loperamide dose on label

**DON'T:**
- Never combine loperamide with cimetidine
- Don't abuse loperamide (serious cardiac risk)
- Don't ignore heart palpitations

**Critical**: This interaction can be fatal. Always use safer alternatives.

## Interaction 7: Dextromethorphan & Diphenhydramine

**Severity**: MODERATE  
**Drug Classes**: Cough Suppressant + Sedating Antihistamine  
**Key Risk**: Additive drowsiness, impaired function

### Active Ingredients to Enter
- Drug 1: `Dextromethorphan` (DXM, cough suppressant)
- Drug 2: `Diphenhydramine` OR `Chlorpheniramine`

### Context
Very common - both are in "nighttime" cough/cold formulas. People may not realize they're getting double sedation.

### Pharmacological Mechanism
**The Interaction**: Additive CNS Depression

Both suppress central nervous system activity, leading to compounded sedation effects.

### Clinical Risks

**1. Enhanced Drowsiness**
- Significant sleepiness
- Dizziness and lightheadedness
- Impaired daily function

**2. Safety Concerns**
- Slowed reaction times
- Impaired motor coordination
- **Increased accident risk**
- Dangerous for driving

**3. Cognitive Effects**
- Confusion (especially in elderly)
- Poor concentration
- Memory issues

### Expert Recommendations

**DO:**
- Be aware that "nighttime" formulas often contain both
- Read all ingredient labels
- Don't add separate sleep aids or allergy pills

**DON'T:**
- Don't drive after taking combination products
- Don't add alcohol
- Don't take additional sedating medications

**Better Approach**: Treat individual symptoms with single-ingredient products.

## Interaction 8: Omeprazole & Iron Supplements

**Severity**: MODERATE  
**Drug Classes**: PPI/H2 Blocker + Mineral Supplement  
**Key Risk**: Chronic reduced iron absorption

### Active Ingredients to Enter
- Drug 1: `Omeprazole`, `Famotidine`, or other acid reducers (PPIs/H2 blockers)
- Drug 2: `Iron` or `Ferrous Sulfate`

### Context
Long-term acid reducer use (for GERD/reflux) combined with iron therapy for anemia. More subtle than antacid interaction but equally problematic.

### Pharmacological Mechanism
**The Interaction**: Sustained pH Elevation

**Unlike Antacids**:
- PPIs/H2 blockers prevent acid production (not just neutralize)
- Create persistently elevated stomach pH (24+ hours for PPIs)
- Iron cannot convert to absorbable form
- Chronic malabsorption

### Clinical Risks

**1. Chronic Treatment Failure**
- Anemia doesn't improve despite taking supplements
- Ongoing fatigue, weakness
- Pale skin, shortness of breath

**2. Masked Problem**
- Subtle interaction - no immediate symptoms
- Can continue for months/years undetected
- Doctors may investigate complex causes while missing simple drug interaction

### Expert Recommendations

**DO:**
- **Discuss with doctor** - requires medical management
- May need IV iron or different formulation
- Consider periodic "acid reducer holidays" if medically safe
- Regular blood tests to monitor iron levels

**DON'T:**
- Don't assume spacing doses will work (PPIs last 24+ hours)
- Don't continue without medical supervision
- Don't ignore persistent anemia symptoms

**Medical Solution**: Doctor may prescribe iron formulation less dependent on stomach acid.

## Interaction 9: Senna & Lactulose

**Severity**: MODERATE  
**Drug Classes**: Stimulant Laxative + Osmotic Laxative  
**Key Risk**: Severe diarrhea, dehydration, electrolyte imbalance

### Active Ingredients to Enter
- Drug 1: `Senna` OR `Bisacodyl` (stimulant laxatives)
- Drug 2: `Lactulose` OR `Polyethylene Glycol` (osmotic laxatives)

### Context
Severe constipation leads someone to take two different types of laxatives simultaneously, hoping for faster relief.

### Pharmacological Mechanism
**The Interaction**: Additive/Overlapping Laxative Effects

**Different Mechanisms**:
- **Stimulant**: Irritates intestines → forceful contractions
- **Osmotic**: Draws massive water into colon → softer, bulkier stool

**Combined**: Overwhelming laxative effect - intense stimulation + watery stool = explosive result

### Clinical Risks

**1. Severe Diarrhea**
- Urgent, explosive bowel movements
- Prolonged diarrhea
- Painful abdominal cramping
- Loss of bowel control

**2. Dehydration** (SERIOUS)
- Large fluid loss
- Dizziness and weakness
- Confusion
- Reduced urination
- Dark urine

**3. Electrolyte Imbalance**
- Loss of potassium, sodium
- Muscle weakness/spasms
- **Abnormal heart rhythms**
- Can be life-threatening

### Expert Recommendations

**DO:**
- Choose ONE laxative type
- Allow adequate time to work (6-12 hours for stimulants, 1-3 days for osmotics)
- Drink plenty of water
- Be patient

**DON'T:**
- **Never combine different laxative types** (unless doctor-directed for medical procedure)
- Don't take additional dose if first doesn't work immediately
- Don't ignore severe diarrhea or dehydration signs

**If Constipation Persists**: See a doctor rather than escalating laxative use.

## Interaction 10: Antacids & Aspirin

**Severity**: LOW to MODERATE  
**Drug Classes**: Antacid + NSAID  
**Key Risk**: Reduced aspirin absorption and effectiveness

### Active Ingredients to Enter
- Drug 1: `Antacid`
- Drug 2: `Aspirin` OR `Acetylsalicylic Acid`

### Context
Person takes aspirin for headache, then takes antacid to prevent/treat stomach irritation aspirin causes.

### Pharmacological Mechanism
**The Interaction**: pH-Dependent Absorption

**Aspirin Chemistry**:
- Weak acid (acetylsalicylic acid)
- Requires acidic stomach for optimal absorption
- Acid environment allows aspirin to cross stomach lining into bloodstream

**Antacid Effect**:
- Neutralizes acid → alkaline environment
- Aspirin cannot be absorbed efficiently
- Drug passes through without working

### Clinical Risks

**1. Reduced Effectiveness**
- Less pain relief
- Inadequate fever reduction
- May lead to taking more aspirin (overdose risk)

**2. Delayed Action**
- What absorption occurs is slower
- Takes much longer to feel relief

### Expert Recommendations

**DO:**
- **Separate by 1-2 hours**: Take aspirin first, antacid later
- Consider enteric-coated aspirin (though timing still matters)
- Use paracetamol instead (gentler on stomach, not affected by antacids)

**DON'T:**
- Don't take together
- Don't take extra aspirin because it's "not working"
- Don't assume coated aspirin is immune to this interaction

**Better Alternative**: If you need both, use paracetamol (acetaminophen) for pain relief instead of aspirin.

## Important Notes

### Exact Ingredient Names
For the app to detect interactions, you must enter active ingredients EXACTLY as shown above (case-insensitive). Common variations:

- ✅ `Ibuprofen` or `ibuprofen`
- ✅ `Iron` or `Ferrous Sulfate` or `ferrous sulfate`
- ✅ `Antacid` or `antacid`
- ✅ `Aspirin` or `Acetylsalicylic Acid` or `acetylsalicylic acid`

### When in Doubt
- Always read the "Active Ingredients" section on medication labels
- Ask your pharmacist
- Use the app to check before combining medications
- When interactions are detected, follow the recommendations or consult healthcare provider

### Emergency Signs
Seek immediate medical help if you experience:
- Vomiting blood or coffee-ground material
- Black, tarry stools
- Severe chest pain or heart palpitations
- Difficulty breathing
- Severe confusion or loss of consciousness
- Signs of severe dehydration
